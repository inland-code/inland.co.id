Water stains on inside walls, wet carpet near the windows, or seepage every time it rains hard—these are classic signs of **leaking building glass**. The good news is that most cases come from one fixable source: **the sealant**.

## What Sealant Does on Building Glass

Sealant is an elastic material that fills the joints between glass, aluminium frames, and facade panels. It keeps out water and air while accommodating small building movements. On glass facades there are two main types:

- **Structural silicone sealant:** helps hold the glass panels in certain facade systems.
- **Weatherproof sealant:** protects joints from rain and temperature changes.

## Why Building Glass Leaks

### 1. UV exposure
Constant sunlight ages sealant, making it lose elasticity and then crack.

### 2. Thermal expansion and contraction
Glass, aluminium, and sealant expand at different rates as temperatures change. This repeated stress weakens the sealant's bond.

### 3. Age
Sealant has a service life—generally 5 to 30 years depending on type, quality, and environment.

### 4. Poor installation
Dirty surfaces, insufficient thickness, or a sealant unsuited to the materials can drastically shorten its life.

### 5. Building movement
Vibration, settlement, or structural changes put extra stress on the joints.

## Signs the Sealant Needs Replacing

- Seepage or water stains around the glass on the inside.
- Sealant that is cracked, hardened, or discoloured.
- Sealant that bubbles or comes away from the glass or aluminium.
- Mould or moss growing along the joint lines.

## The Fix: Proper Re-Sealing

1. **Inspection** to confirm the source of the leak.
2. **Removal of the damaged old sealant.**
3. **Cleaning and surface preparation** so the new sealant bonds properly.
4. **Backer rod and primer** where needed.
5. **Application of new sealant** with the right thickness and profile.
6. **Testing** once the sealant has cured.

On multi-storey buildings this work is done most efficiently with [rope access](/en/what-is-rope-access/), without scaffolding.

## Silicone or Another Type of Sealant?

For exterior glass facades, **silicone sealant** is usually chosen because it resists UV and temperature changes and lasts longer than many other types. Even so, the product must match the joint's function:

- Joints that help hold glass panels need **structural silicone** to the facade system's specification.
- Weather-sealing joints use a **weatherproof sealant** compatible with glass and aluminium.
- Porous materials such as stone or concrete need a suitable sealant and primer to avoid staining.

Always follow the manufacturer's specification and a facade specialist's advice, especially for structural joints.

## Partial or Full Re-Sealing?

- **Local repair** suits leaks limited to a few spots where the rest of the sealant is still elastic.
- **Full re-sealing** is better when sealant in many areas is hardened, cracked, or old—otherwise new leak points will keep appearing.

A close inspection (by rope access) helps decide the most cost-effective option.

## Preventing Repeat Leaks

1. Schedule sealant inspections together with routine glass cleaning.
2. Make sure gutters, roof drainage, and drains don't overflow onto the facade.
3. Use experienced applicators who follow proper surface preparation.
4. Keep a record of the date and type of sealant used to plan future maintenance.

## Don't Put Off the Repair

Small leaks can damage ceilings, walls, carpets, and even electrical installations. Sealant checks are best scheduled alongside routine [building window cleaning](/en/building-window-cleaning-cost/).
